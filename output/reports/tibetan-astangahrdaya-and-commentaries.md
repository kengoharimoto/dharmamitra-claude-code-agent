Text extents are confirmed by the Derge chapter colophons [D]:

- **D4310** has six sthānas. It ends with *phyi ma'i gnas kyi le'u bzhi bcu* (U.40) and *rgyud phyi ma'i gnas te drug pa* (D4310:335a-28–32).
- **D4311** is complete, Sū.1 to U.40. Vol. *he* ends with Sū.14 (*dvividhopakramaṇīya*, D4311:410a-29). Vol. *e* is headed *rang 'grel smad dum* ("lower half of the auto-commentary"). It opens with Sū.15 (*śodhanādigaṇa*, 1b-1) and ends with U.40 (421a-13).

- **D4312**, in three parts:
  - *ko* = Sū.1–29, ending in the colophon of *le'u nyi shu rtsa dgu pa*.

  - *go* (*smad dum*) = Ci.15 to U.40. It opens with *udara* (*dmu rdzing*) and ends with the U.40 commentary and the author's colophon.

## 2. Who translated what, and in what order

### 2.1 Two translation campaigns

1. **The Western Tibet (Gu ge) campaign under Ye shes 'od** (late 10th c. / turn of the 11th) produced the *Vaiḍūryakabhāṣya* (D4311).
   - The translators were Dharmaśrīvarman and Śākya blo gros, with revision by Rig pa gzhon nu and Dbyig gi rin chen [D].
   - Jayapaṇḍita's *Thob yig* (via Taube 1981: 18–21) states that this translation was made **before** that of the root text [M].
   - Zur mkhar ba Blo gros rgyal po goes further. He says the *AHS together with its autocommentary* was first translated by this team (cited by van der Kuijp, *Za hor II*, Journal of Tibetology 12, 2016 [W]).
2. **Rin chen bzang po (958–1055) with the Kashmiri (?) paṇḍita Jarandhara/Janārdana** translated the root (D4310) and Candranandana's commentary (D4312), c. 1013–1055 (Taube: 1012–1039/40) [M].

### 2.2 What the Derge text itself shows

These points come from the Derge text [D], plus the DharmaNexus match layer:

- **D4311 contains its own, independent translation of the root verses.** Compare the opening verse (Sū.1.1):
  - D4310: *'dod chags la sogs ma lus pa / rgyun du 'brel pas lus kun ma lus khyab*
  - D4311: *'dod chags la sogs ma lus pa yi nad / rgyun du 'brel pas lus la kun tu khyab*

  Compare also Sū.1.2:
  - D4310: *tshe ni ring par 'dod pa yis / chos dang nor dang bde ba bsgrub*
  - D4311: *tshe ring ldan par 'dod pa na / chos dang nor yang bde bar sgrub*

  Across the whole text, DharmaNexus finds only **150 parallels between D4310 and D4311(-2), with a maximum score of 88–92**. These are related renderings, not a shared text.
- **D4312's lemmata are D4310's text.**
  - D4312 reads *lus kun ma lus khyab*, the D4310 wording, not D4311's.
  - DharmaNexus finds **2,953 parallels between D4310 and the three parts of D4312**, many of them at score 100.
  - [I] So the lemmata in the Padārthacandrikā were filled in from the Rin chen bzang po root translation, which is what one expects from a single team doing both.
- **D4311 ↔ D4312**: only 43 parallels. The two commentaries are independent.
- [I] Taken together, this fits the *Thob yig*/Zur mkhar ba account of an earlier, separate rendering of the verses inside D4311.

### 2.3 Colophon details worth noting

- **D4310 says *bsgyur cing zhus te gtan la phab*.** That means Rin chen bzang po and Jarandhara both translated and revised.
  - The rKTs transcription of the Peking/Narthang/Golden colophon reportedly has only *zhus te gtan la phab*, which would imply a revision of an existing version.
  - Derge does **not** support that reading. Whether Q/N/G really lack *bsgyur cing* needs checking on the scans before anyone builds an argument on it.
- **"Mar lo" is not a separate reviser.** The D4311 colophon reads *mar lo dge slong rig pa gzhon nu dang / mnyel bcor dge slong dbyig gi rin chen dang gsum gyis* (D4311-2:421a-25). The "three" can only be Śākya blo gros, Rig pa gzhon nu and Dbyig gi rin chen.
  - This supports Rechung (1973: 18), who reads "Mar lo Rig pa gzhon nu" as one person, against Meulenbeld's three-name listing (HIML IA: 683; IB n.577–578).
  - *mnyel bcor* (Q *snyel sbyor*, N/G *snyel por*) is a further epithet of Dbyig gi rin chen. *i gu* qualifies Śākya blo gros.

## 3. The author's name and his father

- **pha khol** is Vāgbhaṭa (often misprinted *pha gol*). Vogel (1965: 45) took it to presuppose a vernacular *Vāgbhaṭṭa*, "father's servant". The Mongolian *Ečige-yin boyol* agrees [M].
- **The father's name: two forms, two texts.** Meulenbeld (IA: 616) notes that Huth reported both *seng ge sbas pa* (Siṃhagupta) and *dge 'dun gsang ba* (Saṅghaguhya/Saṅghagupta), while Vogel recorded only the former. Searching the full Derge texts with the DharmaNexus `find` endpoint resolves this [D]:
  - **D4310 (root)** names the father only once, in the final colophon (335a-31): **dge 'dun gsang ba**. The Sanskrit equivalent is Saṅghagupta, a variant attested in some Sanskrit MSS of the Hṛdaya and by the Hṛdyākāra [M].
  - **D4311 (bhāṣya)** names him at five sthāna-ends (56b-3, 152a-16, 274b-18, 292a-28, 421a-15), always as **seng ge sbas pa** (Siṃhagupta).
  - So the two forms come from two different translation campaigns, which implies two different Sanskrit exemplars. That is presumably why Huth, working across the Tengyur, saw both.
- **Is the bhāṣya an "auto-commentary"?** The vol. *e* heading calls it *rang 'grel*, and Meulenbeld follows that (IA: 683, n.579). However, the bhāṣya's own introduction speaks of Vāgbhaṭa in the third person: *phyis sman pa chen po pha khol zhes bya bas … btus pa … sman dpyad kyi gzhung bris so* (D4311:335b-31).
  - [I] This does not exclude authorship by Vāgbhaṭa, since Indian authors do refer to themselves this way. But the *rang 'grel* label looks like a later Tibetan editorial heading, and the attribution should be treated as open.
  - Content-wise, D4311 is a fluent prose paraphrase rather than a gloss commentary. See for example its rendering of the drug *gaṇas* of Sū.15 (D4311-2:1b ff.).

## 4. Candranandana: new data from the Tibetan colophons

The closing verses of D4312 (*go* vol., 304a-5–32) [D] say the following:

- **Grandfather:** *dpal chen dga' ba* ("renowned; even today the learned proclaim his stainless fame"). This corresponds to Meulenbeld's **Mahānandana** [M, IIA: 121].
- **Father:** *chags la dga'*, and in D4309 *chags la mngon par dga'*.
  - Meulenbeld gives "Ravinandana or Ratinandana" (IIA: 121). The Tibetan *chags (pa)* translates **rati/rāga**, not *ravi* (= nyi ma).
  - **The Tibetan therefore supports Ratinandana** [I, but a strong one].
- **Self-description:** *slob dpon blo gros chen po*, a physician bearing a name "meaningful like Ātreya's" (*rgyun shes don ldan ming btags pa*), called *don dam* (?), and **born in Kashmir** (*kha che'i yul du skyes pa yin*).
  - This colophon is the basis of the "Tibetan tradition" that Candranandana was Kashmirian [M, IIA: 121].
  - Klebanov (INDOLOGY, Oct. 2019 [W]) considers the Kashmir link weak.
- **The king:** a benediction on *rgyal po dpa' ldan tha gan*. Kapstein (same thread) reads this as "Tha ga na" and could not identify the king. [I] Possibly a Kashmiri *Ṭhakkana*. Unresolved.
- **The patron/teacher in D4312's opening verses:** *dpal ldan sgrib med lha*. The Sanskrit says the work was written at the instance of **Śakunadeva** [M]. The Tibetan does not obviously render that name, and this needs comparison with the Sanskrit (Sūtrasthāna ed. 1928).
- **The maṅgala to Hari** survives in translation: *'phrog byed mi zad tshig gi gter / lha yi lha dbang rab btud de* (Hari = 'phrog byed; *devadeveśa*). The translators placed their own homage to Bhaiṣajyaguru Vaiḍūryaprabha before it (D4312:1b-5–8).
- **D4309 is Candranandana's *Madanādinighaṇṭu*.**
  - Its opening is *chags la mngon par dga' ba'i bu zla ba dga' ba … thams cad mkhyen la phyag 'tshal te / yan lag brgyad pa las bstan pa'i sman sde po son cha la sogs …*. Here *po son cha* = **madana**, so *madanādi* is the first drug group of Sū.15.
  - The maṅgala to **Sarvajña** matches what Meulenbeld reports for the Sanskrit nighaṇṭu (IIA: 120–121).
  - The Tibetan canon thus ascribes the nighaṇṭu to the same Candranandana as the Padārthacandrikā. That bears on P.V. Sharma's "two Candranandanas" thesis, which Meulenbeld already doubted.
  - D4309 names no translator.
- **Date:** the only hard terminus is Rin chen bzang po's translation (before 1055). Klebanov adds that Kṣīrasvāmin (11th c.) quotes him. Emmerick and Huth suggested a link to the 8th-century "Candradeva" of the G.yu thog biography, which is legendary [M, W]. Most current opinion places him in the 10th century (van der Kuijp: "?10th c.").
- **The Sanskrit text is incomplete.** Only the Sūtrasthāna of the Padārthacandrikā is edited (1928). **The Tibetan D4312 is therefore the only complete witness of the commentary.** As of January 2026 nothing further had appeared (INDOLOGY query by D. Reigle [W]).

## 5. Religious framing added by the translators

- All four Tibetan texts open with a translator's homage to **Bhaiṣajyaguru Vaiḍūryaprabharāja** before the Indian maṅgala (D4310:44b-6; D4311:335b-5; D4312:1b-6; D4309:13b-5) [D].
- The AHS itself already mentions Bhaiṣajyaguru: there is a mantra in Sū.18, and his epithets Tathāgata, Samyaksambuddha and Vaiḍūryaprabharāja occur [M, IA: 603–604].
- [I] The title *Vaiḍūryakabhāṣya* probably plays on the same epithet.
- The same Bhaiṣajyaguru opening frames the Rgyud bzhi. Its full epithet string (*… dgra bcom pa yang dag par rdzogs pa'i sangs rgyas sman gyi bla …*) matches the D4311/D4312 form, not D4310's shorter one. It is the standard tathāgata formula, though, so this proves nothing about dependence (see §6.3).
- Vāgbhaṭa's "Buddhist" readings (*Jina/Jinasuta*, Ci.19.98; Āryāvalokita, U.5.50) are discussed by Meulenbeld (IA: 603–604). [I] Checking which readings the Tibetan presupposes, against Candranandana's *Śivasuta = Gaṇeśa*, would be a worthwhile small study.

## 6. Relationship to the Rgyud bzhi

### 6.1 Earlier scholarship

- Emmerick (1977) found at least one stanza identical in the Rgyud bzhi and the Tibetan AHS (Sū.16.46). Meulenbeld notes close chapter-level correspondences, which emerge once one allows for the Rgyud bzhi's 9-syllable lines against the AHS's 7-syllable lines. He leaves the direction of dependence open [M, IA: 656].
- **Yang Ga (2010, Harvard PhD; summarised in Hofer ed. 2014)** makes these proportions directly or indirectly AHS-derived [W, via Gerke, JIABS 35]:
  - about **¼ of the Bshad rgyud**
  - about **7% of the Man ngag rgyud**
  - about **3% of the Phyi ma'i rgyud**

  Van der Kuijp gives his overall figure as "almost 20%" of the Rgyud bzhi taken over or recast, and calls this conservative.
- Martin (2007) argues that the Tibetan AHS dominated Tibetan medicine until the Rgyud bzhi rose in the 13th century [W].

### 6.2 Full census: the Derge AHS (D4310) against the Rgyud bzhi

**Data.**
- **D4310** is complete: 218,247 syllables, with all 120 chapter colophons located (Sū 30, Śā 6, Ni 16, Ci 22, Ka 6, U 40). It comes from DharmaNexus.
- **The Rgyud bzhi** comes from the MonlamAI e-texts. Coverage:
  - *Rtsa rgyud*: complete (4,417 syllables).
  - *Bshad rgyud*: complete (28,773).
  - *Phyi ma'i rgyud*: complete (37,664).
  - *Man ngag rgyud*: **chapters 1–27 of 92 only** (57,958).
- **The *Man ngag* figures are therefore partial.** Its chapters 28–92 were not available anywhere on this machine. They include the eye, children's, possession, poison and rejuvenation chapters, which correspond to much of the AHS Uttarasthāna.

**Method.** Everything is Wylie-normalised and compared syllable by syllable. Scripts and outputs are listed at the end of this section.
- *Verbatim:* exact shared strings of ≥6 syllables. Strings occurring more than 3 times in either text are dropped as stock phrases.
- *Fuzzy:* a Rgyud bzhi line counts as AHS-related when a set share of its distinctive syllable bigrams occurs in a single D4310 window of comparable length. Bigrams frequent in either corpus are excluded.
- *Calibration:*
  - Spot-checks show that ≥50% matches are mostly genuine recasts.
  - At ≥40% the matches are mixed.
  - At ≥30% they are dominated by coincidental vocabulary, so I do not use that level.
  - A random-window control gives 0% at every threshold. That rules out chance, but not shared topical vocabulary.

**Share of each tantra related to the Tibetan AHS:**

| | Rtsa | Bshad | Man ngag (ch. 1–27) | Phyi |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Verbatim (≥6-syllable exact strings) | 1.8% | 3.2% | 0.5% | 1.1% |
| Fuzzy, conservative (≥50%) | 2.6% | 10.4% | 1.2% | 1.4% |
| Fuzzy, liberal (≥40%) | 9.0% | 17.9% | 4.2% | 4.5% |
| Yang Ga, "directly or indirectly derived" | n/a | ~25% | ~7% (whole tantra) | ~3% |

**How to read these numbers:**
- **The ranking matches Yang Ga's.** The *Bshad rgyud* is far more dependent than the other tantras.
- **My absolute figures are lower,** as they should be: a lexical measure cannot see content that was reorganised or re-expressed. Treat the liberal fuzzy figures as a floor for "recast" material, and the verbatim figures as a floor for "copied" material.
- **Metre explains the thin verbatim layer.** The Tibetan AHS is in 7-syllable lines and the Rgyud bzhi in 9-syllable lines, so most borrowed lines had to be recast.
  - The one long verbatim passage, **Emmerick's verse** (36 syllables; *Bshad* ch.16), is the chapter-final verse of Sū.16. There D4310 itself switches to **9-syllable lines** (D4310:81b-20–23) for the longer closing metre, so it could be taken over unchanged:

    > *skyes bu snum la brten par byed pa rnams / nang gi drod 'bar khong pa dag pa dang / lus zungs 'phral brta stobs dang mdog bzang ldan / dbang brtan rgas sra lo brgya thub par bshad*

    The only difference is *brta/brtas*. [I] This is very likely why Emmerick found exactly this verse.
- **Overall:** 218 verbatim passages. 210 are single lines (6–11 syllables) and 8 are multi-line.

**Where the borrowing sits.** The verbatim and fuzzy measures agree. Rgyud bzhi chapters are numbered as in the Rgyud bzhi itself.

### 6.3 Which Tibetan AHS does the Rgyud bzhi follow: D4310 or the Gu ge bhāṣya?

**Test (a): look up each D4310↔Rgyud bzhi passage in D4311.** I checked all 218 verbatim passages.
- **69: D4311 has the same verse in different wording.** Here the Rgyud bzhi follows D4310 *against* D4311. Examples:
  - Sū.2 → *Bshad* 13. D4310 and Rgyud bzhi: *gnas 'gro zhes rtag tu dran pa nyer 'jog*. D4311: *ji lta bur gnas shing 'gro zhes rtag tu sems 'di ltar dran*.
  - Sū.11 → *Bshad* 11. D4310 and Rgyud bzhi: *mig ser bkres skom lus tsha gnyid chung*. D4311: *skom dad che lus tsha gnyid chung ba*.
  - Sū.8 → *Bshad* 18. D4310 and Rgyud bzhi: *zas tshod ma zin chung gyur na stobs*. D4311: *zas tshod ma zin te ha cang cher zos pa*.
- **19: identical in all three.** All stock material: names of sages (*drang srong chen po rgyun shes kyi bu*), *chos dang nor dang bde ba*, colophon formulae.
- **130: not found in D4311.** The bhāṣya paraphrases or skips the verse.

**Test (b): the converse.** I looked for strings the Rgyud bzhi shares with D4311 but not with D4310.
- After removing colophon formulae, drug lists joined by *dang*, and the tathāgata homage, 84 n-grams remain, in 60 short passages.
- On inspection they are **all prose stock phrases or drug-name pairs** (*'bras bu gsum dang rtsa ba lnga*, *pho ba'i me drod skyed par byed*). None is a distinctive verse line.
- The homage is a curiosity: the Rgyud bzhi's opening epithet string (*… dgra bcom pa yang dag par rdzogs pa'i sangs rgyas sman gyi bla …*) matches D4311 and D4312, not D4310's short form. But it is the standard tathāgata formula and proves nothing.

**Test (c): the verse pairs DharmaNexus aligns between D4310 and D4311.**
- Of the 436 aligned pairs, only about 9 are verses the Rgyud bzhi actually reuses.
- The one clear case is the stages of intoxication (Sū.5 → *Bshad* 16). There the Rgyud bzhi's *gnas la gnas rnam rtog ngan bcom … bde ba yin snyam sems* matches D4310's distinctive wording 9:0 against D4311's prose.

**Conclusions.**
- **The verbatim AHS layer in the Rgyud bzhi is the verse translation in D4310 (Jarandhara / Rin chen bzang po),** not the renderings of the Ye shes 'od-era bhāṣya.
- [I] Two explanations are possible:
  - the Rgyud bzhi, in the redaction we have, drew on D4310; or
  - the two share a lost Tibetan verse version. Zur mkhar ba says the AHS itself, not just the bhāṣya, was first translated under Ye shes 'od. If so, that version would have to be one that D4310 then took over largely unchanged.
- What the evidence does exclude is that the Rgyud bzhi's AHS material came through the Gu ge translation **as preserved in D4311**.
- **On direction, lexical overlap cannot decide** between "Rgyud bzhi used D4310" and the reverse. [I] The metrical pattern points one way, though. Recasting 7-syllable lines into 9 everywhere, except where D4310 is already 9-syllabic, is the natural behaviour of a redactor working *from* D4310. The reverse would require Rin chen bzang po to have trimmed Rgyud bzhi lines to 7 syllables while leaving exactly the chapter-final 9-syllable verse intact. That is a much less economical hypothesis.
- This fits Yang Ga's and van der Kuijp's view of a post-1050 Rgyud bzhi redaction drawing on the Tengyur AHS. It sits badly with the traditional 8th-century dating of the Rgyud bzhi in its present form.
- **Caveat:** D4311's "renderings" are verses embedded in a prose commentary, often paraphrased. If the Gu ge campaign produced a separate verse translation of the root, it does not survive independently, so this test cannot rule it out as a common ancestor.
- **Later quotation follows D4310 too.** Stag tshang's quotations (§7) use D4310's wording (*tshe ni ring par 'dod pa yis*), not D4311's.

## 7. Later Tibetan reception visible in the corpus

From the DharmaNexus `table --agg --all` survey of D4310 [D]:

- **Stag tshang lo tsā ba Shes rab rin chen** (b. 1405), *Rig gnas kun shes nas bdag med grub pa'i rnam bshad* (BO_S10_TAGT003).
  - The medicine section quotes the **Tengyur AHS**, not the Rgyud bzhi. It cites Sū.1.2–3, the fourfold *catuṣpāda* (Sū.1.27), the disease list, and dinacaryā verses explicitly as *gzhung 'di'i mdo gnas le'u gnyis pa nyin re spyod pa'i le'u*.
  - [I] For Stag tshang, *gso ba rig pa* as one of the five Indian sciences meant Vāgbhaṭa.
- **A Sa skya gong ma work** (BO_S09_ZUCN178D, a kāvya-style survey of the śāstras, sarga 4) quotes the AHS origin-of-Āyurveda verses (Sū.1.3ff.).
- **Sa skya Paṇḍita's bka' 'bum** lists a *Yan lag brgyad pa'i snying po* (dpe bsdur ma vol. 25: 147–189) [W]. It is presumably a digest; I have not seen it.
- **Rejuvenation (bcud len) manuals** cite *yan lag brgyad par* for the Gaṅgā-water verse (Sū.5.1): BO_S07_DBTU292, and Gdams ngag mdzod TSA-019.
- **A Yuan print:** blocks carved in Dadu in 1311 for a *Yan lag brgyad kyi snying po*, which van der Kuijp identifies as the AHS (+ bhāṣya), not the Rgyud bzhi [W].
- **Si tu Paṇ chen** consulted Sanskrit MSS of the AHS and Candranandana in 1724 [W, van der Kuijp].
- **Sde srid Sangs rgyas rgya mtsho** quotes D4312 as *Zla zer* in the *Bai ḍūrya sngon po* (Gerke) [W].
- **An annotated separate print:** Leh, T. P. Emchi 1985 (Smanrtsis Shesrig Spendzod 143), with *mchan* by an unknown author [W].

## 8. Gaps in the DharmaMitra/DharmaNexus coverage

- **No cross-language alignment.** The Sanskrit AHS (SA_GS40_vagaah_u, linked richly to the Saṃgraha, Cakradatta, Bhāvaprakāśa, Suśruta and others) has **no** precomputed parallels to D4310. Semantic `/primary/` search from the Sanskrit Sū.1.1 does not retrieve the Tibetan either. A Sanskrit↔Tibetan alignment of D4310 would be a high-value addition to the corpus.
- **No metadata.** Menu metadata (`--meta`) for these five files is null.
- **No Rgyud bzhi** in the DharmaNexus corpus (not found by `identify-text.py`).

## 9. The Tibetan against the surviving Sanskrit

### 9.1 Method

- **Root text.**
  - *Sanskrit:* the Das & Emmerick e-text of the AHS (1997, GRETIL). It has 120 chapters, the same structure as the Tibetan.
  - *Tibetan:* D4310, where nearly every DharmaNexus segment is one 7-syllable pāda.
  - *Alignment:* within each chapter, monotone verse-to-span alignment. One or two Sanskrit verses match 1–8 consecutive Tibetan lines.
  - *Scoring:* the Dharmamitra cross-lingual sentence-alignment embedder (`mitra-qwen35-2b-embedder-sentence-alignment`).
  - *Calibration:* true matches score a median cos of 0.92, against about 0.80 for the best wrong span nearby.
  - *Classes:* close ≥ 0.85, loose 0.78–0.85.
  - *Structure check:* the median is 4.1 Tibetan lines per Sanskrit verse, so a śloka normally becomes four Tibetan pādas.
- **Padārthacandrikā.**
  - *Sanskrit:* only the Sūtrasthāna survives. I used the archive.org OCR layer of the 1928 edition, which prints Aruṇadatta, Hemādri and Candranandana together, and extracted the 916 blocks marked `प०` (Candranandana's siglum).
  - *Tibetan:* D4312 Sū.1–30 (vol. *ko* plus the start of *kho*), split into 1,269 lemma-and-gloss blocks.
  - *Alignment:* one monotone sequence over the whole Sūtrasthāna, allowing a block on one side to match up to three on the other.
- Scripts and full outputs are in `output/reports/data/tibetan-ahs-sanskrit-alignment/`.

### 9.2 Root text: the Tibetan is a close, nearly complete translation

| Sanskrit verses | Count | In the Tibetan | Absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular verses | 7,418 | **98.9%** (92.5% close, 6.4% loose) | 63 (0.8%) |
| Lines Das & Emmerick print as additions (`n+1` etc.) | 226 | 31% | **67%** |
| ™-marked verses | 46 | 100% | 0 |

- **Tibetan side.** 98% of D4310's lines align to Sanskrit verses. Most of the rest is colophons the Sanskrit e-text doesn't print: sthāna ends, the endings of each of the eight *aṅgas* (*byis pa gso ba'i yan lag rdzogs so* …), and the final author colophon. The rest are the standard chapter-opening formulae (*athāta … vyākhyāsyāmaḥ / iti ha smāhur ātreyādayo maharṣayaḥ*), which the e-text also omits.
- **Genuine Tibetan additions are under 1% of the text.** They are mainly short recipe and drug-list lines (Sū.15, Ci.1, Ci.3, Ci.5, U.5, U.24) and one line on massage after exercise (Sū.2).
- **The added verses.** Two-thirds of the lines Das & Emmerick print as additions from other editions are not in the Tibetan. Rin chen bzang po's exemplar was therefore close to the shorter text. One clear exception is the Bhaiṣajyaguru mantra (Sū.18.17+1, *namo bhagavate bhaiṣajyagurave vaiḍūryaprabharājāya*), which the Tibetan transliterates.
- **Readings.** I tested 383 lexically substantive pāda variants in the Das & Emmerick apparatus.
  - Where the embedding can decide, the Tibetan follows the base text **84 times** and the variant **9 times**. The other 290 are undecided, because an embedding cannot separate near-synonyms.
  - The spot-checked verdicts are correct in both directions. Three Tibetan-supported variants:
    - Sū.6.111d *kāsān hanty asrapittakṛt*: *khrag mkhris skyed*.
    - U.26.24d *sīvanyā picuyuktayā*: *ras bal dang bcas pa'i khab kyis*.
    - Ci.9.114b *palāṃśakam*: *srang ni re re*.

### 9.3 What the Tibetan leaves out

Only three passages of the regular text are clearly absent: no match anywhere in the chapter. Their content is telling.

- **Ci.7.53cd–97: wine for pleasure (43 verses).** The Tibetan renders Ci.7.53ab, *rig par chang 'thung gang gis ni / nad ni 'byung bar 'gyur ma yin* (*yuktamadyasya madyottho na vyādhir upajāyate*). It then jumps straight to Ci.7.98, *de la rlung nad la phan chang* (*tatra vāte hitaṃ madyam*) (D4310:187a-6–8). Two things are missing:
  - the panegyric of wine as bearer of the Aśvins' splendour, Sarasvatī's strength, Indra's vigour and Viṣṇu's power (54 ff.);
  - the ornate description of drinking for pleasure.

  The therapeutic use of wine, by doṣa, is kept.
- **Sū.4.33: rites.** *atharvavihitā śāntiḥ pratikūlagrahārcanam / bhūtādyasparśanopāyo* (Atharvavedic pacification, worship of hostile planets) is missing. Sū.4.32 and 4.34 on either side are translated.
- **Ci.19.98: worship as a cure for kuṣṭha.** The verse prescribing vows, worship of brahmins, gods and teachers, and adoration of Śiva/Jina, his son, Tārā and the Sun is missing. The Tibetan chapter goes from 97 straight to its colophon (D4310:227b-44–46).

**This is selective, not random.** The Tibetan keeps:
- the Hindu lineage of Āyurveda (Brahmā, Prajāpati, the Aśvins, Indra in Sū.1.3: *tshangs pa, skye dgu'i bdag, tha skar, brgya byin*);
- the Buddhist figures (Āryāvalokiteśvara in U.5.50, rendered *'phags pa spyan ras gzigs dbang phyug, mgon po phyag bcu gnyis pa*);
- the mantras (Vaiḍūryamātā and Gaurī/Gāndhārī in U.35.29–30, transliterated).

What is absent is the celebration of alcohol and two prescriptions of non-Buddhist worship.

[I] That fits a choice by monastic translators, but it cannot be proven from the Tibetan alone. Ci.19.98 is missing from some Sanskrit manuscripts too (Meulenbeld IA: 604), so the exemplar may already have lacked it. The Ci.7 and Sū.4.33 gaps would need checking against the manuscript tradition.

A further 17 single verses scored as absent, but each has a strong match (0.83–0.90) elsewhere in its chapter. They are most likely alignment artefacts, not omissions.

### 9.4 Padārthacandrikā (Sūtrasthāna)

**Chapter-level correspondence is complete.** Every Sanskrit chapter maps onto the corresponding Tibetan chapter, Sū.1–30. Of the 916 Sanskrit blocks, 911 fall into aligned positions. For 424 (46%) the embedding independently picks the aligned Tibetan block as the best local match; the rest are placed by sequence. No run of Sanskrit commentary lacks Tibetan.

**The opening is absent from the Sanskrit.** The Tibetan begins with Candranandana's introductory verses and the gloss on Sū.1.1 (D4312:1b–3a): the homage to Hari, the patron *dpal ldan sgrib med lha*, and the lineage. The 1928 edition, as scanned, prints Aruṇadatta's and Hemādri's comments on Sū.1.1 but no `प०` block before verse 2. Either the edition never printed that section or this copy lacks it. Either way, the Tibetan is the only source here for the opening (cf. §4).

**Limits.** The OCR is too noisy for passage-level claims.
- Correct pairs score a median cos of 0.83, against 0.74 for wrong pairs from the same chapter (90th percentile 0.81), so individual matches are uncertain.
- The Tibetan has about 1.4 times as many blocks. That is partly the verse-by-verse lemma format, but a strict alignment still leaves about a quarter of the Tibetan blocks unmatched. Most of those inspected are sequence conflicts rather than missing Sanskrit.

A clean re-OCR of the 1928 edition would allow the same verse-level comparison as for the root text.
